Star and Exoplanet Research Group (SERG)

Research

Each of our research areas starts from the same kind of data: precise measurements of how a star’s brightness changes with time. What differs is the physics hiding in the light curve.

Eclipsing binary stars

When two stars orbit in a plane lined up with our view, each periodically blocks the other’s light. The depth, width and shape of these eclipses tell us the orbital inclination, the relative sizes and temperatures of the stars, and, for the closest pairs, their mass ratio.

Much of our work focuses on contact binaries, or W UMa-type stars: two stars so close that they share a common envelope of gas. We combine multi-colour (BVRI) CCD photometry from ground-based telescopes with light curves from NASA’s TESS mission, model the systems with the Wilson–Devinney code, and follow their orbital periods over years to decades using eclipse timings.

Questions we are asking

  • How do contact binaries with very low mass ratios evolve, and how close are they to merging into a single star?
  • What do slow changes in orbital period reveal about mass transfer between the stars, angular momentum loss and unseen companions?
  • How well can photometry alone determine a binary’s mass ratio, and why are totally eclipsing systems so valuable for this?

Transiting exoplanets

A planet passing in front of its host star blocks a small fraction of the starlight, typically around 1% for a Jupiter-sized planet orbiting a Sun-like star and far less for smaller worlds. Measuring these transits gives the planet’s radius, orbital period and orbital inclination.

We analyse transit light curves from TESS and ground-based follow-up observations. Our experience with eclipsing binaries is directly useful here: blended or grazing eclipsing binaries are among the most common impostors of planetary transits, and recognising them is an essential part of confirming a planet.

Questions we are asking

  • Which transit candidates are genuine planets, and which are eclipsing binaries in disguise?
  • How precisely can a planet’s size and orbit be measured from the shape of its transit?
  • Can small shifts in transit times reveal additional planets in the same system?

Stellar flares and activity

Flares are sudden releases of magnetic energy in a star’s atmosphere. On cool red dwarfs, the most common stars in the Galaxy, flares can briefly outshine the whole star at blue and ultraviolet wavelengths. TESS’s continuous, high-cadence photometry has made it possible to catch thousands of these events.

We detect flares in time-series photometry, measure their energies and durations, and study how often stars of different types flare. Because many nearby rocky planets orbit red dwarfs, this work also bears on whether those planets can keep their atmospheres.

Questions we are asking

  • How often do cool stars flare, and how much energy do their flares release?
  • How does flaring activity change with a star’s rotation and age?
  • What does repeated flaring mean for the atmospheres and habitability of nearby planets?

How we work

Students in the group learn the full path from raw observations to a published result.

  • Ground-based photometry

    Multi-colour CCD observations, calibration and differential photometry to build light curves.

  • Space photometry

    Light curves from NASA’s TESS mission, with nearly continuous coverage over weeks.

  • Modelling

    Binary light-curve modelling with the Wilson–Devinney code, transit fitting and eclipse-timing (O–C) analysis.

  • Scientific computing

    Python for data reduction, statistics and visualisation, with reproducible analysis.
